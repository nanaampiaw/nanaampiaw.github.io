Engineering, Ethics, and Societal Impact

The more I build, the more specific my questions about engineering and society become. Who will use the system? What happens when a sensor gives an incorrect reading? Can the person using it understand what it is doing? A design can meet its technical objective and still leave important questions unanswered.

The smart sustainable home project made these tradeoffs concrete. Our team explored motion-based control of lights and fans, a time-of-day control model, and an app for monitoring household electricity use. Automation offered a way to reduce unnecessary consumption, but it also raised questions about user control. I want energy-saving systems to help people make informed choices and to remain understandable when their needs change.

The project also gave me a reason to be careful about how I describe results. Our energy analysis projected a reduction in consumption; we did not install a complete system and measure savings in an occupied building. That distinction matters to me. A prototype can demonstrate an idea, while a claim about actual performance needs evidence from the conditions in which the system will operate.

During my garage-door internship, safety sensors and programmed stop limits gave me another way to think about responsibility. These settings affect the behavior of moving equipment. Assisting with their calibration connected control-system concepts to the need for consistent, dependable operation around people.

My background in Ghana also influences how I think about access to technology. When considering an energy solution, I want to ask about the equipment, the resources needed to maintain it, and the people who will depend on it. I’m learning to carry those questions into technical work rather than treating them as something to consider only after a design is complete.
